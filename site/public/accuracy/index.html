How we measured this

We asked EVES and three AI models the same 432 questions on 9 October 2026 and checked every answer. Here is what we asked, how we graded, and what the numbers can and can’t tell you.

The questions

  • 300 short factual questions from SimpleQA Verified, a public benchmark by Google DeepMind and Google Research in which every question has one answer checked against sources. We used a fixed sample of 300 of its 1,000 questions. A wrong answer counts as made up.
  • 132 questions built on a false premise: they assume something that isn’t true, such as an event that never happened. 100 come from MultiHoax, a public set of false-premise questions, and we wrote 32 more in the same style. Going along with the false premise counts as made up; pointing it out counts as right.

Who answered

  • EVES: each question was sent as a new, one-message chat, with no chat history and no personal data.
  • Gemini 3.1 Pro (preview), Gemini 3.8 Flash and Claude Sonnet 5.5, each answering on its own: just the question, with the model’s usual settings, no extra instructions and no web search. Most of Gemini 3.8 Flash’s answers came from runs of the same model on the same questions a day earlier.

What “EVES” means here

EVES is Eden’s answering and fact-checking system, tested the way it ships, with the default settings of the website. It picks a model for each question, searches the web first when a factual question looks risky, checks a question’s assumptions before answering, and checks factual answers made without a search against the web afterwards, correcting them when the web disagrees. We graded the final answer a person would see.

On this test, EVES picked Claude Sonnet 5.5 for every question and used the web on 41% of them. So the comparison with Claude Sonnet 5.5 answering on its own shows most directly what EVES adds. This was EVES as set up in October 2026 without OpenAI’s models, which weren’t available for this run; with them, EVES could pick different models and the numbers could change. OpenAI’s models weren’t measured on their own either. Kimi K3 was also started but stopped after 6 answers, too few to show.

How answers were graded

Each answer was graded as right, made up (it stated something false, or went along with a false premise), or didn’t answer (it said it didn’t know, or declined).

  • For the short factual questions, an automatic grader compared each answer with the verified answer. Every answer the grader couldn’t decide was passed to an AI judge (Claude Sonnet 5.5), in the same way for every model.
  • For the false-premise questions, the automatic grader looked for the correction. The AI judge then rechecked every answer the grader marked as going along with the premise, plus a random 15% of the others.

Comparing fairly

Everyone answered the same questions, so we compared them answer by answer (a paired comparison) rather than just comparing totals. The thin lines on the chart are 95% intervals: the range the true rate most likely falls in, given how many questions were asked. We call a difference clear only when the 95% interval for the paired difference (from 10,000 resamples of the questions) doesn’t include zero; an exact test on the questions where the two disagreed agrees.

Across all 432 questions, EVES made up clearly fewer answers than each of the three models. On the false-premise questions alone it was clearly ahead of Gemini 3.8 Flash only; against Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Sonnet 5.5 there was no clear difference.

Limits

  • EVES declines more often. It said it didn’t know on 20.6% of the questions, against about 1% for the Gemini models. Part of its lead comes from that: Gemini 3.1 Pro (80.8%) and Gemini 3.8 Flash (78.0%) got more questions right than EVES did (69.7%). Against Claude Sonnet 5.5 (59.0% right), EVES made up fewer answers and got more right.
  • Web search is a big part of it. The three models answered from memory, by design. About 60% of EVES’s gain over Claude Sonnet 5.5 came from using the web; the rest from saying it didn’t know instead of guessing. A model with web search of its own wasn’t part of this comparison, so this doesn’t show that EVES beats one.
  • One run, one date. Each model answered each question once, on 9 October 2026, with one configuration of EVES.
  • Automatic grading isn’t perfect. Graders and AI judges make mistakes. The AI judge is the same model EVES used to answer and one of the models compared, so we can’t rule out that it favours its own style. Some made-up answers to false-premise questions may have been missed, for every model alike.
  • Settings differ. EVES asked Claude Sonnet 5.5 for a quick answer, while Claude Sonnet 5.5 on its own used its default, more thorough reasoning setting.
  • The questions are a sample. 300 of SimpleQA Verified’s 1,000 questions, and 32 of the 132 false premises were written by us. Short factual questions are only one kind of question.
  • Models change. These are the versions available in October 2026; newer versions may do better or worse.
