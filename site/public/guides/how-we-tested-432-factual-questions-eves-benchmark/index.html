Inside the 432-Question Hallucination Benchmark: EVES vs. Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.8 Flash & Claude Sonnet 5.5

A transparent look at how we evaluated 300 SimpleQA Verified factual questions and 132 false-premise questions, why confidence intervals and abstention rates matter, and where verification helps most.

Published 2026-10-21 · 9 min read · By Eden (Harrat Global Holdings, Inc.)
View the Full Interactive Chart Explore EVES

Marketing benchmarks in AI often cherry-pick tasks where a vendor’s system wins and hide the caveats in footnotes. When we built EVES (Eden’s verification and evaluation system), we wanted a benchmark that tested two things critical to real-world professional and academic work: hard factual recall and resistance to false premises.

The 432-question evaluation suite

  • 300 short factual questions from SimpleQA Verified (Google DeepMind & Google Research, MIT licence): unambiguous, fact-seeking questions where parametric memory often fails.
  • 132 false-premise questions (100 from MultiHoax / MHFPQ, CC BY 4.0, plus 32 written by us): questions containing a fabricated premise designed to test whether the system points out the false assumption or plays along.

Four caveats we publish alongside every chart

  1. EVES declines more often: EVES abstained on 20.6% of questions (vs. <1% for the Gemini models). Both Gemini models answered more questions right overall than EVES did, because they guessed on almost every question—getting many right, but also fabricating nearly twice as often.
  2. Web search accounts for ~60% of the gain over Claude Sonnet 5.5: The three baseline models answered from memory without web search.
  3. Single run on a fixed date: Measured on 9 October 2026 without OpenAI models enabled in that EVES configuration.
  4. Automated & judge grading limits: Graded by rule and an AI judge; see askeden.com/accuracy for the full protocol.

Frequently asked questions

Why is "Made something up" separated from "Said it didn’t know"?

Because in high-stakes work—medicine, law, finance, and university coursework—an honest "I cannot verify this" is safe, whereas a confident fabrication causes real damage.

Where can I inspect the exact grading rules and datasets?

Visit askeden.com/accuracy#method for the full dataset citations (SimpleQA Verified and MHFPQ), confidence interval formulas, and judge rubrics.
